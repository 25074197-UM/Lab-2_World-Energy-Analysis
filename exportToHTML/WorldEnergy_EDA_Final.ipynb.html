# World Energy EDA: Economic Development and Environmental Cost (2000–2024) 
 
--- 
 
## Research Problem Statement 
 
Despite rapid economic growth in the 21st century, it remains unclear whether wealthier nations are successfully decoupling GDP growth from greenhouse gas emissions, or whether higher economic output continues to drive proportionally higher energy consumption and environmental impact — a gap that critically undermines the formulation of effective climate policy and sustainable development targets. 
 
--- 
 
## Research Questions 
 
**RQ1:** Is there a statistically significant positive relationship between a **country's GDP** and its **primary energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions** over the period 2000–2024? 
 
**RQ2:** Do the **top 10 highest-GDP countries** show a different **emissions-to-energy trend** compared to the **bottom 10 lowest-GDP countries** over the same period? 
 
--- 
 
## Research Objectives 
 
1. To perform systematic data quality assessment on the World Energy dataset and justify the selection of the 2000–2024 scope based on data completeness evidence. 
2. To identify the strongest correlated variable triplet (GDP, energy, emissions) as the analytical scope through exploratory correlation analysis. 
 
--- 
 
## EDA Workflow 
 
``` 
Raw data (23,195 rows × 130 cols) 
    │ 
    ├─ Step 1: Load & Inspect 
    ├─ Step 2: Data Quality Checks 
    ├─ Step 3: Classify entities (country vs aggregate) 
    ├─ Step 4: Scope selection — Time period justification 
    ├─ Step 5: Scope selection — Variable selection via correlation 
    ├─ Step 6: Data cleaning & imputation 
    ├─ Step 7: Post-clean validation 
    ├─ Step 8: Univariate analysis 
    ├─ Step 9: Bivariate & relationship analysis 
    ├─ Step 10: Group comparison (Top 10 vs Bottom 10 GDP) 
    └─ Step 11: Summary & conclusions 
```

## Step 4: Time Period Scope Selection — Justification 
 
**Rationale:** The full dataset spans 1900–2024 (124 years), but data quality degrades significantly before 2000. We use evidence from the data itself to justify restricting to **2000–2024**. 
 
Three lines of evidence support this decision: 
 
1. **Greenhouse gas emissions data** is entirely absent before 2000 (100% missing pre-2000). 
2. **GDP data** is ~49% missing pre-2000 vs ~30% post-2000 — already borderline, but usable. 
3. **Primary energy consumption** is 57.6% missing pre-2000 vs only 1.8% post-2000. 
 
Any analysis spanning pre-2000 years would require imputing majority-missing columns — introducing more noise than signal. The **2000–2024 window** is the largest range with acceptable data completeness for all three core variables.

## Step 6: Data Cleaning & Imputation 
 
**Rationale:** With the scope defined, we now clean only the three selected columns. The cleaning sequence matters: 
 
1. **Sort by country + year** — ensures interpolation fills gaps chronologically, not randomly. 
2. **First-pass interpolation** — linear interpolation within each country's time series (limit=2 gaps). This fills small within-series gaps using the adjacent years. 
3. **Zero/negative value replacement** — zero energy consumption or negative GDP are physically/economically impossible and are treated as encoding errors, replaced with NaN. 
4. **Second-pass interpolation** — re-interpolate after zero replacement (no limit — remaining gaps after step 2 are structural, not encoding errors). 
5. **Drop rows still missing** — countries with no recoverable data for any of the three variables are excluded. 
 
> **Fix from Makai's notebook:** The order of operations was wrong — zeroes were handled *after* the first drop, meaning some countries were dropped before their zero values were corrected. Also, `limit=1` in first interpolation was too conservative given some countries have 2-year reporting gaps. 
>  
> **Fix from Notebook A:** Cleaning was flagged but never executed. Here it is fully implemented. 
 
#%% 
# ── Build the working DataFrame ─────────────────────────────────────────────
ID_COLS  = ['country', 'iso_code', 'year']
TRIO     = ['gdp', 'primary_energy_consumption', 'greenhouse_gas_emissions']

df_work = rc_scoped[ID_COLS + TRIO].copy()
print(f"Working DataFrame shape (before cleaning): {df_work.shape}")
print()
print("Missing values before cleaning:")
print(df_work[TRIO].isnull().sum().to_string())
#%% 
# ── Step 6a: Sort by country + year ─────────────────────────────────────────
# Critical: interpolation assumes chronological order within each country group
df_work = df_work.sort_values(['country', 'year']).reset_index(drop=True)
print("✅ Sorted by country + year.")
#%% 
# ── Step 6b: First-pass interpolation (limit=2 consecutive gaps) ────────────
# 'limit=2' handles countries with up to 2-year reporting gaps (e.g. small island nations)
# 'limit_direction=both' fills gaps at the start and end of a series too
for col in TRIO:
    df_work[col] = (df_work
                    .groupby('country')[col]
                    .transform(lambda x: x.interpolate(
                        method='linear', limit_direction='both', limit=2)))

print("Missing after first-pass interpolation:")
print(df_work[TRIO].isnull().sum().to_string())
#%% 
# ── Step 6c: Replace zero and negative values with NaN ─────────────────────
# Zero or negative values are physically impossible for these variables:
#   - GDP cannot be 0 or negative (would indicate no economic activity)
#   - Primary energy consumption cannot be <= 0
#   - Greenhouse gas emissions <= 0 would imply net carbon sink (not in this dataset context)

zero_counts = {}
for col in TRIO:
    n_zero = (df_work[col] <= 0).sum()
    zero_counts[col] = n_zero
    df_work.loc[df_work[col] <= 0, col] = np.nan

print("Zero/negative values replaced with NaN:")
for col, n in zero_counts.items():
    print(f"  {col:<40}: {n} values replaced")
#%% 
# ── Step 6d: Second-pass interpolation (no limit — structural gaps only) ────
for col in TRIO:
    df_work[col] = (df_work
                    .groupby('country')[col]
                    .transform(lambda x: x.interpolate(
                        method='linear', limit_direction='both')))

print("Missing after second-pass interpolation:")
print(df_work[TRIO].isnull().sum().to_string())
#%% 
# ── Step 6e: Drop rows with any remaining missing values ────────────────────
rows_before = len(df_work)
df_clean = df_work.dropna(subset=TRIO).copy()
rows_after = len(df_clean)

print(f"Rows before drop : {rows_before:,}")
print(f"Rows after drop  : {rows_after:,}")
print(f"Rows removed     : {rows_before - rows_after:,}")
print(f"Countries retained: {df_clean['country'].nunique()}")

    ly = np.log10(df_plot[ycol])
    mask = np.isfinite(lx) & np.isfinite(ly)
    z = np.polyfit(lx[mask], ly[mask], 1)
    p = np.poly1d(z)
    x_range = np.linspace(lx[mask].min(), lx[mask].max(), 200)
    ax.plot(10**x_range, 10**p(x_range), 'r--', linewidth=2,
            label=f'Trend: slope={z[0]:.2f}')
    ax.legend(fontsize=9)

    r_val = np.corrcoef(np.log10(df_plot[xcol][mask]), np.log10(df_plot[ycol][mask]))[0,1]
    ax.set_title(f'{xcol.upper().replace("_"," ")} vs {ycol.upper().replace("_"," ")}\n'
                 f'Pearson r (log-log) = {r_val:.3f}', fontweight='bold')
    plt.colorbar(sc, ax=ax, label='Year')

plt.suptitle('Bivariate Relationships — GDP vs Energy & Emissions (2000–2024)\n'
             'Colour = Year: yellow dots above trendline suggest later-year outliers',
             fontsize=12, y=1.02)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

## Step 10: Group Comparison — Top 10 vs Bottom 10 GDP Countries 
 
**Rationale:** This addresses **RQ2**: *Do the top 10 highest-GDP countries show a different emissions-to-energy trend compared to the bottom 10?* 
 
**Method:** We rank countries by their **average GDP across 2000–2024** (not a single-year snapshot, which could be biased by a crisis year). Countries are then grouped as Top 10 or Bottom 10, and we compare their distributions and time-series trends for all three variables. 
 
**Why average GDP instead of a single year?** A country may have an unusual GDP in any given year (oil boom, financial crisis). The 25-year average is a more stable measure of economic standing. 
 
> **Makai's notebook was correct on this logic.** Added here with additional chart types: time-series trend lines and a normalised emissions-intensity view.
